# Cell Biology

Revision notes for the cell biology unit. Each section ends with the key definitions to learn for the exam.

## 1. Cell theory

Cell theory has three parts. All living things are made of one or more cells. The cell is the basic unit of structure and function in living things. All cells arise from pre-existing cells.

The theory grew out of microscopy. Robert Hooke described "cells" in cork in 1665, though what he saw were the empty walls of dead plant cells. Antonie van Leeuwenhoek was the first to observe living single-celled organisms in the 1670s. Matthias Schleiden concluded in 1838 that all plants are made of cells, and Theodor Schwann extended this to animals in 1839. Rudolf Virchow added the third part in 1855 with the phrase "omnis cellula e cellula": every cell comes from a cell. This finally replaced the idea of spontaneous generation, which Louis Pasteur's swan-neck flask experiments disproved experimentally in 1859.

Exceptions worth knowing for essay questions: striated muscle fibres contain many nuclei and are far longer than a typical cell; fungal hyphae are not divided into separate cells; giant algae such as Acetabularia are single cells several centimetres long. Viruses are not cells at all, which is one reason they are not usually considered living.

## 2. Prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells

Prokaryotic cells (bacteria and archaea) are small, typically 1 to 10 micrometres across. Their DNA is a single circular chromosome lying in a region of the cytoplasm called the nucleoid, with no nuclear envelope around it. Many also carry small extra loops of DNA called plasmids, which often hold genes for antibiotic resistance. Prokaryotes have 70S ribosomes, a cell wall (made of peptidoglycan in bacteria), and may have a capsule, pili and a flagellum. They have no membrane-bound organelles.

Eukaryotic cells (animals, plants, fungi and protists) are larger, typically 10 to 100 micrometres. Their linear chromosomes are enclosed in a nucleus, they have 80S ribosomes in the cytoplasm, and they are divided into compartments by membrane-bound organelles. Plant cells add a cellulose cell wall, chloroplasts and a large permanent vacuole; animal cells have none of these but do have centrioles.

The endosymbiotic theory explains where mitochondria and chloroplasts came from: they were once free-living prokaryotes engulfed by a larger cell. The evidence is that both have their own circular DNA, 70S ribosomes, a double membrane, and divide by a process like binary fission.

Definition, prokaryote: a single-celled organism whose DNA is not enclosed in a nucleus and which has no membrane-bound organelles.

## 3. Organelles

The nucleus holds the cell's chromosomes. It is surrounded by a double membrane, the nuclear envelope, which is perforated by nuclear pores that let mRNA out to the cytoplasm. Inside, the nucleolus is where ribosomal RNA is made and ribosome subunits are assembled.

Ribosomes are made of rRNA and protein and are the site of protein synthesis. Some float free in the cytoplasm and make proteins used inside the cell; others are attached to the rough endoplasmic reticulum.

The rough endoplasmic reticulum (rough ER) is a network of flattened membrane sacs studded with ribosomes. Proteins made there are folded and transported in vesicles, usually to the Golgi apparatus. The smooth ER has no ribosomes; it makes lipids and steroids and, in liver cells, detoxifies drugs.

The Golgi apparatus is a stack of flattened sacs that modifies proteins (for example by adding carbohydrate to make glycoproteins), then sorts and packages them into vesicles for secretion or for delivery to other organelles. Lysosomes are vesicles from the Golgi that contain hydrolytic (digestive) enzymes at a pH of about 4.5 to 5; they break down worn-out organelles and material taken in by phagocytosis.

Mitochondria are the site of aerobic respiration. Each has a double membrane: the inner membrane is folded into cristae to increase the surface area for the electron transport chain, and it encloses a fluid matrix where the link reaction and Krebs cycle happen. Mitochondria have their own circular DNA and 70S ribosomes. Cells with high energy demands, such as muscle and sperm cells, contain large numbers of them.

Chloroplasts are found in plant and algal cells and are the site of photosynthesis. They have a double membrane, internal membranes called thylakoids stacked into grana, and a fluid called the stroma around them.

The cytoskeleton is a network of protein filaments (microfilaments, intermediate filaments and microtubules) that gives the cell its shape, anchors organelles and moves vesicles and chromosomes.

Definition, organelle: a specialised structure within a cell that carries out a particular function, usually enclosed by a membrane.

## 4. The plasma membrane and transport

The fluid mosaic model (Singer and Nicolson, 1972) describes the plasma membrane as a phospholipid bilayer about 7 nanometres thick, with proteins scattered through it like tiles in a mosaic. The phospholipids' hydrophilic phosphate heads face the watery environment on each side, and their hydrophobic fatty acid tails face inwards. The structure is fluid because individual phospholipids and many proteins can move sideways. Cholesterol sits between the phospholipids and regulates fluidity, making the membrane less fluid at high temperatures and stopping it becoming rigid at low ones. Glycoproteins and glycolipids on the outer surface act in cell recognition and as receptors.

Substances cross the membrane in several ways.

Simple diffusion moves small, non-polar molecules such as oxygen and carbon dioxide straight through the bilayer, down their concentration gradient. Its rate rises with a steeper concentration gradient, a larger surface area, a higher temperature and a thinner exchange surface (Fick's law).

Facilitated diffusion moves ions and polar molecules such as glucose down their concentration gradient through channel proteins or carrier proteins. Like simple diffusion it is passive: it uses no ATP.

Osmosis is the movement of water across a partially permeable membrane. In a hypotonic solution an animal cell takes in water and may burst (lysis), whereas a plant cell becomes turgid because its wall resists expansion. In a hypertonic solution an animal cell shrinks (crenation) and a plant cell becomes plasmolysed as its membrane pulls away from the wall.

Active transport moves substances against their concentration gradient using carrier proteins and energy from ATP. The best-known example is the sodium-potassium pump, which moves 3 sodium ions out of the cell and 2 potassium ions in for every ATP it hydrolyses. Root hair cells use active transport to take up mineral ions from the soil.

Bulk transport moves large particles in vesicles, and also needs ATP. Endocytosis takes material into the cell (phagocytosis for solids, pinocytosis for liquids); exocytosis releases material, such as secreted hormones and enzymes, from it.

Definition, diffusion: the net movement of particles from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration, without the use of energy from ATP.

Definition, osmosis: the net movement of water molecules from a region of higher water potential to a region of lower water potential across a partially permeable membrane.

Definition, active transport: the movement of molecules or ions against their concentration gradient through carrier proteins, using energy from ATP.

## 5. Enzymes

Enzymes are globular proteins whose tertiary structure creates an active site, a pocket with a shape and chemistry complementary to the substrate. When a substrate binds it forms an enzyme-substrate complex, the reaction happens, and the products are released, leaving the enzyme unchanged and free to catalyse again.

The lock and key model treats the active site as a rigid shape that only the right substrate fits. The induced fit model (Koshland, 1958) is the accepted refinement: the active site changes shape slightly as the substrate binds, which strains the substrate's bonds and helps the reaction happen.

Factors affecting the rate of an enzyme-controlled reaction:

- Temperature: the rate rises as molecules gain kinetic energy and collide more often, up to an optimum of about 37 degrees Celsius for most human enzymes. Above the optimum, vibrations break the hydrogen and ionic bonds holding the tertiary structure, the active site changes shape and the enzyme is denatured.
- pH: each enzyme has an optimum pH. Pepsin in the stomach works best at about pH 2; most enzymes in the cytoplasm work best near pH 7. Far from the optimum, the charges on the active site change and the enzyme can denature.
- Substrate concentration: the rate rises until every active site is occupied, when the enzyme is working at its maximum rate (Vmax).
- Inhibitors: a competitive inhibitor resembles the substrate and competes for the active site, so adding more substrate overcomes it. A non-competitive inhibitor binds elsewhere on the enzyme (an allosteric site), changing the active site's shape; adding more substrate does not overcome it.

Definition, enzyme: a biological catalyst, usually a protein, that speeds up a reaction without being used up.

Definition, activation energy: the minimum energy needed for a reaction to start. Enzymes speed reactions up by lowering it.

## 6. Cellular respiration

Aerobic respiration releases energy from glucose in four stages. Overall: glucose + oxygen gives carbon dioxide + water, or C6H12O6 + 6O2 → 6CO2 + 6H2O.

Glycolysis happens in the cytoplasm and needs no oxygen. One glucose is phosphorylated and split into two molecules of pyruvate. It uses 2 ATP and makes 4, so the net yield is 2 ATP per glucose, plus 2 reduced NAD (NADH).

The link reaction happens in the mitochondrial matrix. Each pyruvate is decarboxylated (releasing carbon dioxide) and oxidised, then combined with coenzyme A to form acetyl coenzyme A. Per glucose it runs twice, giving 2 NADH and 2 carbon dioxide.

The Krebs cycle also happens in the matrix. Acetyl CoA joins a four-carbon compound to form a six-carbon one, which is broken back down to four carbons, releasing carbon dioxide. Per glucose the cycle turns twice and makes 2 ATP, 6 NADH, 2 FADH2 and 4 carbon dioxide.

Oxidative phosphorylation happens on the inner mitochondrial membrane. NADH and FADH2 hand their electrons to the electron transport chain; as the electrons pass along it, their energy pumps protons into the intermembrane space. Oxygen is the final electron acceptor and combines with electrons and protons to form water. The protons flow back into the matrix through ATP synthase, which uses their energy to make ATP; this coupling is called chemiosmosis. Altogether, aerobic respiration yields about 30 to 32 ATP per glucose, most of it from oxidative phosphorylation.

Without oxygen, only glycolysis can continue, producing 2 ATP per glucose. To regenerate NAD, animal cells reduce pyruvate to lactate; yeast and plants convert it to ethanol and carbon dioxide (alcoholic fermentation).

Definition, glycolysis: the first stage of respiration, in the cytoplasm, which splits one glucose molecule into two pyruvate molecules with a net gain of 2 ATP.

## 7. Photosynthesis

Photosynthesis stores light energy as chemical energy in glucose: 6CO2 + 6H2O → C6H12O6 + 6O2, using light energy absorbed by chlorophyll. It has two stages.

The light-dependent reactions happen on the thylakoid membranes. Light excites electrons in chlorophyll in photosystem II; they pass along an electron transport chain to photosystem I, and their energy is used to make ATP by chemiosmosis (photophosphorylation). The electrons finally reduce NADP to NADPH. The electrons lost by photosystem II are replaced by photolysis, the splitting of water, which releases oxygen as a by-product.

The light-independent reactions, also called the Calvin cycle, happen in the stroma. The enzyme RuBisCO fixes carbon dioxide by combining it with the five-carbon compound ribulose bisphosphate (RuBP). The product is reduced to triose phosphate (G3P) using ATP and NADPH from the light-dependent reactions. Most triose phosphate regenerates RuBP; the rest is used to make glucose, sucrose, starch, amino acids and lipids. Six turns of the cycle make one glucose.

The rate of photosynthesis is limited by whichever factor is in shortest supply: light intensity, carbon dioxide concentration or temperature. Glasshouse growers raise all three to increase yield.

Definition, chlorophyll: the green pigment in chloroplasts that absorbs mainly red and blue light and reflects green light.

## 8. The cell cycle and mitosis

The cell cycle is the sequence of events from one division to the next. Most of it is interphase: in G1 the cell grows and makes proteins and organelles; in the S phase it replicates its DNA, so that each chromosome becomes two identical sister chromatids joined at the centromere; in G2 it grows further and checks the replicated DNA. Then comes M phase: mitosis (division of the nucleus) followed by cytokinesis (division of the cytoplasm).

Mitosis has four phases:

- Prophase: chromosomes condense and become visible, the nuclear envelope breaks down, and spindle fibres form from the centrioles.
- Metaphase: chromosomes line up along the middle of the cell (the metaphase plate), attached to spindle fibres by their centromeres.
- Anaphase: the centromeres split and the spindle fibres pull the sister chromatids to opposite poles.
- Telophase: the chromatids reach the poles, uncoil, and a nuclear envelope forms around each set.

Checkpoints at G1/S, G2/M and in metaphase stop the cycle if the DNA is damaged or chromosomes are not attached correctly. Cancer results when mutations in the genes that control these checkpoints (proto-oncogenes and tumour suppressor genes such as p53) let cells divide uncontrollably.

Mitosis produces two daughter cells that are genetically identical to the parent and to each other, each with the full diploid number of chromosomes. It is used for growth, repair and asexual reproduction.

Definition, mitosis: nuclear division that produces two genetically identical daughter nuclei, each with the same number of chromosomes as the parent nucleus.

## 9. Meiosis

Meiosis makes gametes. It involves two divisions and produces four haploid cells, each genetically different, from one diploid cell. In humans the chromosome number halves from 46 to 23, so fertilisation restores 46.

In meiosis I, homologous chromosomes pair up in prophase I and are separated, halving the chromosome number. In meiosis II, the sister chromatids are separated, as in mitosis.

Meiosis creates genetic variation in two ways. In crossing over, during prophase I, non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes exchange sections of DNA at points called chiasmata, producing new combinations of alleles. In independent assortment, during metaphase I, each homologous pair lines up independently of the others, so the maternal and paternal chromosomes are shuffled into the daughter cells in different combinations: in humans there are 2 to the power 23, about 8.4 million, possible combinations. Random fertilisation adds further variation.

Errors in separation (non-disjunction) produce gametes with an extra or missing chromosome. Down syndrome, trisomy 21, results from an extra copy of chromosome 21.

Definition, crossing over: the exchange of DNA between non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes during prophase I of meiosis.

## 10. DNA replication and protein synthesis

DNA replication is semi-conservative: each new molecule keeps one strand of the original and gains one new strand. Meselson and Stahl confirmed this in 1958 by growing bacteria on heavy nitrogen and tracking the density of their DNA. During replication, the enzyme helicase unwinds the double helix and breaks the hydrogen bonds between bases. Free nucleotides pair with the exposed bases (adenine with thymine, cytosine with guanine), and DNA polymerase joins them, working only in the 5' to 3' direction. The leading strand is made continuously; the lagging strand is made in short Okazaki fragments, which DNA ligase joins.

Protein synthesis has two stages. In transcription, in the nucleus, RNA polymerase copies the base sequence of a gene into a molecule of messenger RNA (mRNA), using uracil in place of thymine. In eukaryotes the introns are then spliced out. The mRNA leaves the nucleus through a nuclear pore.

In translation, at a ribosome, the mRNA is read three bases at a time. Each transfer RNA (tRNA) carries a specific amino acid and has an anticodon that pairs with a complementary codon on the mRNA. The ribosome joins the amino acids with peptide bonds to build the polypeptide. There are 64 possible codons: 61 code for amino acids and 3 are stop codons (UAA, UAG and UGA). AUG is the start codon and codes for methionine. Because several codons code for the same amino acid, the genetic code is described as degenerate.

Definition, codon: a sequence of three bases on mRNA that codes for one amino acid or a stop signal.
